Color Spaces & the Fold Pipeline · Lesson 1

Two ways to put light in a number

Linear vs. gamma — and why the plan's "display space" and "screen space" are the same space.

Every value in the pipeline you designed — a texel, a framebuffer pixel, a blend result — is just a number, usually between 0 and 1. This lesson answers one question: what does that number mean? There are exactly two answers, and almost every color bug in real-time rendering comes from confusing them. Everything else in this course — the depth buffer, the fold, the tonemap — sits on top of this one distinction.

1 · Linear: the number is the light

In a linear encoding, the number is directly proportional to physical light — photons hitting a sensor. 0.5 means half as many photons as 1.0. Double the number, double the light.

This is the encoding that math is correct in. When two lights overlap, their photons add: 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7 worth of light, physically. When you average two pixels (a blur, a downscale, antialiasing), the average is only physically right if the numbers are linear. Lighting equations, shadows, blending real light — all of it assumes linear.

This is the whole thesis of the canonical source for this lesson: GPU Gems 3, Ch. 24, "The Importance of Being Linear." Read it after this lesson — link at the bottom.

2 · Gamma: the number is perceived brightness

In a gamma encoding (the sRGB curve — the one your PNGs, your monitor cable, and PSX framebuffers use), the number is not proportional to light. It is bent by a curve — roughly light = code2.2 to decode, and code = light1/2.2 to encode.

Why bend it? Two reasons, both real:

• Human perception. Your eye is far more sensitive to differences in dark tones than bright ones. The gamma curve spends more of the available code values down in the darks, where you can actually see the steps. With only 8 bits (256 values) per channel, that's the difference between smooth shadows and visible banding.

• History & hardware. CRT monitors physically responded with a ~2.2 power curve, so images were pre-encoded to cancel it. That convention froze into sRGB, and it's still what a display expects at its input today.

The gotcha that trips everyone A gamma 0.5 is not half the light. Decode it — 0.52.2 ≈ 0.21 — and it's only about 21% of full brightness. So adding two gamma numbers is not adding two lights. That single fact is why the engine can't just blend wherever it likes, and it's the seed of your entire fold design.

3 · The knot: "display" vs. "screen"

Here is the thing you flagged as confusing, stated plainly:

Terminology resolution In the design docs, "display space" and "screen space" are two names for the same space — the gamma (sRGB-family) encoding. There is no third or fourth color space hiding here. What differs is not the space but the pipeline stage where that same gamma encoding gets applied.

The prior design notes used two words for one thing, and that's exactly what made it feel like there was a distinction to grasp. There isn't — as color spaces, they're identical. Read them like this:

• "display" — the gamma values used around the fold (the seed → fold → copy-out passes), so the additive PSX blend happens in gamma, the way the original hardware did it.

• "screen" — the gamma values produced at the very end by the tonemap pass, for the monitor.

Same encoding, applied twice, for two different reasons, at two different moments. That's the entire "display vs. screen" mystery. We'll pin down each of those moments, pass by pass, in later lessons — but you now have the key that unlocks them.

This resolution is now a workspace convention. Every later lesson uses "gamma space" as the real name, and treats "display" / "screen" as stage labels on that one space.

4 · Why this is the foundation of your plan

Your engine does lighting in linear (because the math must be right). But the PSX look requires the additive fold to happen in gamma (because that's where the original hardware added its sprites, and adding in linear gives the wrong colors). So the pipeline has to leave linear, do the fold in gamma, and come back:

lighting        the fold (PSX)        back to engine       monitor
linear  ──▶  display (gamma)  ──▶  linear  ──▶  screen (gamma)
          seed A          fold B      out C           tonemap

That's the whole 5-pass diagram in one line. Notice display and screen are the same amber — one space, two stops. The fold is a bracketed gamma excursion inside a linear pipeline. Don't worry yet about who does each arrow or how — that's the rest of the course. Today's win is just: you can read that line and know what every word means.
